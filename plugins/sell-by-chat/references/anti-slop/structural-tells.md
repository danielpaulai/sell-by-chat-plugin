# Structural Tells — Syntax, Rhythm, Logic, Format

Word lists are the easy half. These are the patterns that give writing away
faster than any vocabulary, because they are **rhythmic** — once you hear them
you cannot unhear them, and swapping synonyms does nothing to fix them.

Every entry follows the same shape: **the tell → why the model does it → the
fix → a worked before/after.**

## 1. The negation pivot

**The tell:** "It's not X, it's Y." "This doesn't just save time, it transforms
how you work." "Not only does it build skills, it fundamentally reshapes how you
think." "It's not a tool. It's a movement."

**Why:** the construction hands the model an instant structure and makes an
ordinary claim sound deliberate. It manufactures the *feeling* of a reframe
without doing the work of one. It sets up a strawman nobody held and knocks it
down.

**The frequency signal:** ChatGPT-family models will use this three times in a
400-word piece. A human uses it once, on purpose, when the contrast is real.

**The fix:** state the second half. The first half was never the point.

> **Before:** "This isn't just about writing better posts — it's about building
> a system that compounds."
> **After:** "Every post you write should make the next one easier to write."

> **Before:** "Great copy isn't about clever words. It's about understanding
> your reader."
> **After:** "Copy works when you can name the sentence your reader says to
> themselves at 11pm."

**Related variants to catch:** "No A, no B, just C." · "Less X, more Y." ·
"Forget X. Think Y." · "X? No. Y." · "The problem isn't X. It's Y."

## 2. The staccato triplet

**The tell:** three consecutive short sentences with the same opening word or
the same grammatical shape. "No fluff. No filler. No BS." "Not 50. Not 500.
Fifteen." "One tool. One workflow. One outcome."

Technically: anaphora plus asyndeton. In 2019 it was a punchy device. In 2026 it
is a tic, and on LinkedIn specifically it now reads as a signal that the writer
outsourced the rhythm.

**The fix:** keep the content, kill the drum pattern. Write it as one
conversational sentence.

> **Before:** "No agencies. No retainers. No six-month contracts."
> **After:** "You won't be signing a six-month retainer with an agency for
> this."

> **Before:** "Not 50 founders. Not 500. Fifteen."
> **After:** "I'm going to work with fifteen founders this quarter, and that's
> the whole list."

## 3. The rule of three

**The tell:** grouping in threes reflexively — "efficiency, clarity, and
impact," "bold, innovative, and transformative." The diagnostic version is when
**the third item is a grander restatement of the second**: "it saves time,
reduces errors, and transforms how your organisation thinks about quality."

**Why:** three is the most probable list length in the training data, and the
escalating third item is the same fake-profundity move as the negation pivot.

**The fix:** count the actual things. If there are two, write two. If there are
five, write five. If the third item is a restatement, delete it.

> **Before:** "It's fast, affordable, and it changes the way you work."
> **After:** "It's fast and it costs £29."

**Not a violation:** three items where each carries distinct weight and none is
a summary of the others.

## 4. The em dash pivot

**The tell is density, not existence.** More than two or three em dashes per
paragraph, used to stop a sentence mid-thought and relaunch it at a higher
register — the punctuation equivalent of the negation pivot.

**Defence of the em dash:** it is a legitimate mark with a real job
(parenthetical asides, appositives, interruptions). Writers who have always used
it should keep using it. Abandoning a good tool because a bad writer overuses it
is a bad trade, and restructuring clean sentences to dodge a dash is how you get
stilted prose.

**The fix:** keep dashes doing dash work. Cut the ones doing pivot work.

> **Before:** "The system works — and it works because it removes the one thing
> that always breaks — human memory."
> **After:** "The system works because it doesn't rely on anyone remembering
> anything."

**Threshold:** flag at more than one em dash per 100 words, or more than two in
any single paragraph.

## 5. The participial tail

**The tell:** a sentence that ends with a present-participle clause vaguely
gesturing at importance. "...highlighting the need for better systems."
"...underscoring the importance of consistency." "...reflecting a broader shift
in how teams operate." "...demonstrating the value of early feedback."

**Why:** it adds the *feeling* of having concluded something without concluding
anything. It is the single most reliable filler construction in LLM prose.

**The fix:** cut the tail. Almost always the sentence is complete without it. If
the tail contained the actual point, promote it to its own sentence and make it
say something specific.

> **Before:** "Three clients churned in Q2, highlighting the importance of
> onboarding."
> **After:** "Three clients churned in Q2. All three had skipped the onboarding
> call."

## 6. Symmetric parallelism

**The tell:** every bullet the same length. Every bullet the same grammatical
shape. Every bullet starting with an action verb. Every bullet exactly two
sentences. Perfect visual alignment.

**Why:** the model optimises for local consistency, and symmetry is the most
probable arrangement.

**The fix:** mix bullet lengths drastically. One bullet can be three words.
Another can be three lines. Some can be fragments. Some can be full sentences.
Real notes look uneven because real thoughts are uneven.

> **Before:**
> - Build your content pillars around your expertise
> - Create a calendar that maps to your business goals
> - Write posts that speak directly to your ideal client
>
> **After:**
> - Pick four topics you could argue about at 2am.
> - Map them to the months. Some months get two.
> - Write to one person. Actual person. Name them.

## 7. Uniform paragraph shape

**The tell:** every paragraph opens with a hook or question, runs two to three
sentences of explanation, and closes on a punchline. Every paragraph is the same
length. Paragraphs artificially capped at three or four sentences.

**The fix:** let paragraph length follow the thought. A paragraph can be one
word. Another can run eight sentences because the idea needed eight. Do not end
every paragraph on a landing.

## 8. Rhythm and burstiness

**The measurable version of the above.** Human writing has high *burstiness* —
sentence lengths swing wildly. Machine writing clusters tightly around the mean.

**Targets:**

| Metric | Slop | Human |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence length standard deviation | under 5 words | 8–15 words |
| Burstiness (stdev ÷ mean) | under 0.40 | 0.55–0.95 |
| Shortest sentence | 8+ words | under 5 words |
| Longest sentence | under 25 words | 30+ words |
| Paragraph length variance | uniform | 1 to 8 sentences |

**The fix — rhythm surgery:** find the three longest consecutive sentences and
break one into two. Find a run of short sentences and fuse two with a comma or
a conjunction. Put a three-word sentence somewhere it will land. Read the whole
thing aloud; wherever you run out of breath or your voice flattens, cut there.

**Warning:** do not turn this into a new pattern. "Long, long, short. Long,
long, short." is also a metronome.

## 9. Elegant variation

**The tell:** refusing to repeat a noun, cycling through synonyms instead. "The
platform... the solution... the tool... the offering... the system... the
product."

**The fix:** repeat the word. Using the same noun for the same thing reads as
confidence and clarity. Synonym-cycling reads as a thesaurus with anxiety.

## 10. Overused copy formulas

These formulas are not wrong. They are *exhausted* — recognisable at a glance
because AI reaches for them by default.

**Problem-Agitate-Solve.** ¶1 state problem, ¶2 make it worse, ¶3 solution, ¶4
CTA. The tell is the *agitate* paragraph doing nothing but restating the problem
with more adjectives.

**Before-After-Bridge.** Pain state, pleasure state, the thing that gets you
across. The tell is that the "after" is a fantasy nobody described in those
words.

**AIDA** run mechanically, one paragraph per letter.

**The AI story arc:** "I used to struggle → then I discovered X → now I succeed
→ you can too." Four beats, no mess, no cost, no doubt.

**The fix:** use the formula as a *checklist of what must be present*, not as a
paragraph order. Start in the middle. Put the proof before the promise. Open on
the objection. Put the CTA in the second line if that is where it belongs. The
formula tells you what the reader needs; it does not tell you the sequence.

---

## 11. The forced numbered framework

**The tell:** "The 5-Step Framework for..." · "7 Secrets to..." · "3 Pillars
of..." — always odd, always suspiciously tidy, content bent to fit the number.

**Why:** odd-numbered listicles dominate the training data.

**The fix:** derive the number from the content, then say it plainly. Four
things is four things. And most content that gets forced into pillars is
actually one idea with examples — say the one idea.

## 12. AI logic patterns

Reasoning shapes that expose machine authorship even when the prose is clean.

**False dichotomy setup.** "Most people do X. Successful people do Y."
"Beginners focus on C. Experts focus on D."
→ *Fix:* acknowledge the grey. Most real differences are of degree and context.

**Cause-effect oversimplification.** "If you do X, you'll get Y." "Want Z? Just
do A." Ignores variables and context.
→ *Fix:* name the conditions. "This works when you already have traffic. It
does nothing if you don't."

**Straw man.** "People think [oversimplified belief]... but actually [my
point]." Sets up weak opposition.
→ *Fix:* state the strongest version of the counter-argument, then answer it.

**Linear progress assumption.** "First X, then Y, then Z." Life is not linear
and the model does not understand mess.
→ *Fix:* show the loop, the setback, the part where you went backwards.

**Universal applicability.** "This works for everyone." "Anyone can do this."
→ *Fix:* qualify honestly. "Worked for me twice. Failed once, with a client who
had no list."

**Symmetrical balance.** Every pro matched with a con, every claim hedged into
neutrality. Reads as fair; is actually a refusal to have a position.
→ *Fix:* take the position. Note the strongest objection. Do not pretend the
sides are equal when you do not think they are.

## 13. AI tonal patterns

**Relentless positivity.** Every setback framed as a lesson, every problem as an
opportunity. Nothing is just bad.
→ *Fix:* let some things be bad. "That launch lost money and taught me nothing."

**Emotional distance.** Describing feelings anthropologically rather than
feeling them. "It can be frustrating when..."
→ *Fix:* write from inside it, in the present.

**Over-inclusivity.** "Whether you're a founder or a freelancer..." Tries to
address everyone.
→ *Fix:* one reader. By name if possible.

**Conflict avoidance.** Never disagreeing without a cushion. "While I respect
that view..." Cannot simply say "that's wrong."
→ *Fix:* take the hard stance without apologising for it.

**Uniform enthusiasm.** Same energy level from first line to last. Humans get
bored mid-piece, get sharp again, trail off.

## 14. Formatting tells

**Bold overuse.** Bolding random phrases for emphasis throughout. Bold should
mark one category of thing (e.g. only defined terms, or only the actions), and
should be rare enough to notice.

**Inline-header bullet lists.** Every bullet as `**Bolded mini-header:** then a
sentence.` A recognised marker.
→ *Fix:* use it only when the headers are genuinely a taxonomy. Otherwise write
the bullets as sentences.

**Title Case In Headings** where the publication style is sentence case.

**Emoji as decoration.** 💡 for ideas, 🚀 for growth, ⚡ for tips, 🎯 for goals,
placed after declarative statements, never mid-sentence, always tonally matched.
→ *Fix:* zero emoji, or emoji used oddly and sparingly where a person would
actually use one.

**Hashtag formula.** Always at the end, always three to five, always relevant,
never ironic.

**Perfect line-break rhythm.** Breaks landing at every logical pause, evenly
spaced, always readable.
→ *Fix:* break where the meaning breaks. Sometimes that is awkward. Awkward is
human.

**Horizontal rules and section dividers** used to structure a 300-word post.

**The tidy summary box** at the end of everything.

**Tables where prose would do.** A two-column table with three rows is a
sentence wearing a costume.

## 15. What AI never does

The inverse tells. Their absence, in aggregate, is itself a signal. Use these
with judgement — they are permissions, not requirements, and forcing them
produces its own artificiality.

**Incomplete thoughts.** Machines always finish. People trail off.

**Self-correction in view.** "Actually, that's not quite right." "Let me put it
another way." Showing the thinking, including the wrong turn.

**Mid-piece reconsideration.** Saying one thing, then complicating it three
paragraphs later. Machines maintain consistency; people change their minds while
writing.

**Tangents.** A brief digression that is interesting and not strictly on-topic.

**Real uncertainty.** "I think, but I'm not sure." "Still figuring this out."
"Could be wrong." Machines hedge with structure ("it depends on various
factors"); people hedge with specifics ("I've only tried this twice").

**Unresolved endings.** Not every piece needs a bow. Some end on the open
question because that is where the thinking actually is.

**A caution on typos:** deliberately introducing spelling errors to seem human
is a bad trade. It costs credibility and it is the one "humanising" tactic that
readers experience as sloppiness rather than personality. Vary the rhythm and
the specificity instead.
