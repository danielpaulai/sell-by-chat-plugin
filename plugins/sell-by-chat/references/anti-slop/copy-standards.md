# Copy Standards — The Positive Standard

The blacklist tells you what not to ship. This file tells you what good looks
like, so the rewrite has somewhere to go. Removing slop from an empty draft
leaves you with a shorter empty draft.

## 1. The specificity ladder

Every claim sits on a rung. Slop lives on rungs 1–2. Ship from 4–5.

| Rung | Example |
|---|---|
| 1. Category | "We help businesses grow." |
| 2. Benefit | "We help businesses get more leads." |
| 3. Mechanism | "We rewrite your LinkedIn profile so the right people message you first." |
| 4. Named outcome | "Three of my clients went from zero inbound to four qualified DMs a week." |
| 5. Named outcome, named person, named number, named cost | "Kasia rewrote her headline on a Tuesday. Eleven DMs by Friday. She'd posted nothing new." |

**The concrete count.** Scan the draft and count concretes: numbers, dates,
names, places, prices, physical objects, verbatim quotes. Target at least one
per 100 words for editorial copy, higher for direct response. Below that, the
piece is vapour regardless of how it reads.

**The upgrade move.** Take the vaguest noun in each paragraph and replace it
with the specific thing it stands for:

- "a nice restaurant" → "that Thai place on Beach Road with the broken sign"
- "recently" → "last Tuesday, about 3pm"
- "a client" → "a fintech founder in Lahore with 400 followers"
- "significant growth" → "from 12 to 61 inbound DMs a month"
- "it was stressful" → "I checked the inbox eleven times before 9am"

**The cost rule.** Real stakes beat described stakes. "This cost me $3,000 to
learn." "I wasted eight months." "I nearly lost the account over it." Numbers
attached to pain are the fastest credibility instrument in copy, and they cannot
be faked without lying, which is why they read as true.

## 2. The one-reader rule

Write to one person. Not a segment, not an avatar with a stock photo — one
person you could name, whose situation you could describe in four sentences.

**The test:** could this be sent to a completely different audience without
anyone noticing? If yes, it addresses nobody.

Signals you are writing to one person:
- You use their vocabulary, including the words they use wrong
- You name the objection they actually raise, not the polite version
- You reference the situation they are in this month, not in general
- You skip the parts they already know

Signals you are writing to everyone:
- "Whether you're X or Y"
- Explaining a term your reader uses daily
- Hedging every claim so no segment is excluded
- The piece works for beginners *and* experts

## 3. Hooks that are not tricks

A hook earns the second line. That is the whole job. It is not a slogan, not a
thesis statement, and not a question the reader can answer "no" to and leave.

**What works:**

- **The specific in-progress moment.** "I deleted 340 leads from the CRM this
  morning." Starts mid-action, with a number.
- **The concrete number that shouldn't be true.** "We spent £11,000 on ads and
  got two calls."
- **The verbatim line someone said.** "'Your profile reads like a CV.' That was
  the whole feedback."
- **The admission with a cost.** "I ran the wrong offer for five months."
- **The stated position someone could fight.** "Lead magnets are the reason your
  list doesn't buy."
- **The tension held open.** A first line that creates a gap and a second line
  that widens it rather than closing it.

**What does not work:** rhetorical questions the reader can answer "no" to. Any
opener from `lexical-blacklist.md` §2. Anything that describes what the post is
about instead of starting it. Anything the reader has seen five times this week.

**The rehook.** Line two must do work too, and it must not restate line one.
Line one opens the gap; line two makes the gap matter. If line two could be
deleted, the hook was one line long and the post starts slow.

## 4. Proof

Claims degrade in this order, best to worst:

1. **Named customer with context.** "Used by eight of the Fortune 50." A named
   customer claim with revenue context is the highest-leverage form of social
   proof measured in landing page testing — meaningfully stronger than a logo
   strip or a generic testimonial.
2. **One well-chosen testimonial.** More testimonials do not compound. One
   specific, well-matched testimonial outperforms a wall of them.
3. **A number with a source, date, and denominator.**
4. **A screenshot or artefact.**
5. **A logo strip.**
6. **"Trusted by thousands."** — this is not proof, it is the absence of proof
   wearing proof's clothes.

**Rules:**
- No stat without source and date.
- Round numbers are presumed invented. 80%, 90%, 3×, 10× need a citation or they
  go. Real measurements are 73%, 2.4×, £11,400.
- If the only proof available is weak, use the weak proof honestly rather than
  inflating it. "I've done this twice and it worked both times" is more credible
  than "proven system."
- Never write a testimonial the client did not say.

## 5. Rhythm targets

See `structural-tells.md` §8 for the full metrics. The short version:

- Sentence lengths must swing. At least one under five words, at least one over
  twenty-five, in anything longer than 200 words.
- Standard deviation of sentence length above 8 words.
- Paragraph lengths must vary. Not every paragraph is three lines.
- No three consecutive sentences with the same opening structure.
- Read aloud. Where your voice goes flat, cut.

## 6. Reading level

Target Grade 3–6 for social and direct response. Grade 8–10 for longform and
B2B web copy where the audience expects density.

Low grade level does not mean simple thinking. It means short words carrying
complex ideas. "We reduced onboarding friction through process optimisation" is
grade 14 and says nothing; "We cut onboarding from nine forms to two" is grade 4
and says everything.

**How to get there:** shorter words, not shorter thoughts. Cut nominalisations.
Use verbs. One idea per sentence. Delete adverbs.

## 7. CTA construction

A CTA fails for one of three reasons: the reader does not know what happens
next, the ask is bigger than the trust built, or the ask is a feeling rather
than an action.

**Rules:**

- **Name the action, not the aspiration.** "Comment SYSTEM and I'll send it" —
  not "start your journey."
- **First-person framing outperforms second-person** on buttons. "Get my audit"
  beats "Get your audit."
- **Specificity beats generic.** "See pricing" and "Get my free growth
  assessment" beat "Learn more" — personalised, specific CTAs convert
  substantially better than generic ones across large-scale analysis.
- **Under four words on buttons.**
- **One CTA per piece.** Two asks halve both.
- **Say what happens after the click.** Removes the hesitation that kills most
  conversions.
- **Add a risk reducer where relevant.** "No card needed." "Two minutes." "One
  email, then nothing."
- **Match the ask to the temperature.** Cold traffic gets a small ask. A DM
  three messages deep can carry a call invitation.

**Sticky CTAs do not stack with above-fold CTAs.** In large-scale landing page
testing, a sticky bottom CTA absorbs nearly all of the addressable benefit; the
above-fold CTA on top of it adds almost nothing. Ship one.

## 8. Direct response specifics

**The pain must be theirs, in their words.** Not the category's pain. Pull the
phrasing from actual DMs, call transcripts, or reviews. A pain sentence you
invented reads invented.

**The mechanism is not optional.** Every promise needs a *how*. "We'll get you
leads" is a wish. "We rewrite the headline so the search terms your buyers use
actually match your profile" is a mechanism. Mechanisms are also what make a
claim defensible.

**Handle the real objection.** Every offer has one objection that actually stops
people. Usually price, usually time, sometimes "I've tried this before." Name it
in the copy, in their words, and answer it. Copy that handles three weak
objections and dodges the real one converts worse than copy that handles only
the real one.

**Urgency must have a mechanism.** A date, a cap, a price change, a cohort
closing. State the mechanism. Manufactured urgency is detected and it costs
trust permanently.

**One email, one job.** An email that informs, sells, nurtures, and announces
does none of them.

## 9. Channel standards

**LinkedIn post.** 1,100–1,500 characters unless the content genuinely needs
more. Max two lines per paragraph. Hook and rehook do distinct jobs. No em
dashes if the operator's style forbids them. One CTA. Comment-trigger CTAs
outperform link-in-post CTAs on reach.

**Carousel.** Content slides three lines maximum. Cover and rehook two lines.
CTA slide one to two lines. Every slide earns the swipe — if a slide only
transitions, delete it. Slide count comes from the content; ask if not given.

**Cheatsheet.** Must be genuinely reference-worthy — the test is whether someone
would screenshot it and open it again in three weeks. Symmetry is acceptable
here (it is a table) but the *content* of each row must be specific.

**DM.** The cardinal rule: if you could send it to anyone, send it to no one.
One reference to something only true of this person. Short. No pitch before the
third message unless they asked.

**Email / newsletter.** Subject line does one job: earn the open. Body does one
job: earn the next email. Three subject line options, and none of them a
question the reader can answer "no" to.

**Comment.** Must add something the post did not say. No "great post." No
summary of the post. One specific reaction or one specific extension.

**Ad copy.** The first line must survive being read at 20% attention. Lead with
the concrete.

## 10. The pre-ship read-aloud

The last gate, and the one that catches what the others miss.

Read the whole thing out loud. Then answer:

1. Does this sound like a person talking, or like a TED talk script?
2. Where did my voice go flat? — cut there.
3. Where did I run out of breath? — break there.
4. What did the reader learn that they could not have guessed?
5. Could someone competent disagree with any sentence in this?
6. If I swapped the company name, would anything break?
7. Is the ask specific enough to act on right now?

Any "no" on 4, 5, 6, or 7 is a rewrite, not an edit.
