# Humanisation Playbook — What To Do Instead

Read this after a gate fails, or when a draft is technically clean and still
lifeless. Removing slop is subtraction; this file is the addition.

**The framing that matters:** you cannot humanise a piece that has nothing in
it. Every move below is a way of getting something real onto the page. If you
have no real material, the honest response is to ask for it, not to simulate it.

## 1. Specificity surgery

Find the vaguest noun in every paragraph. Replace it with the actual thing.

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| a nice restaurant | that Thai place on Beach Road with the broken sign |
| recently | last Tuesday, about 3pm |
| a friend | my old flatmate Sarah |
| a client | a fintech founder in Lahore, 400 followers |
| significant growth | 12 inbound DMs a month to 61 |
| it took a while | eleven weeks |
| a popular tool | Notion, on the free plan |
| industry leaders | two agencies in Manchester who charge £8k a month |
| we saw improvement | close rate went from 1 in 9 to 1 in 4 |

**Cultural specificity works the same way:** not "popular music" but the actual
song. Not "a well-known framework" but the name of it. Specificity signals that
you were there.

**The hard constraint:** every specific must be true. Invented specificity is
the worst failure mode in this entire skill, because it survives every stylistic
filter and destroys trust when caught. If you do not have the number, ask for
it or write around it honestly: "I've only run this twice."

---

## 2. Stake injection

Abstract claims become credible when they cost something. Find the sentence
making the biggest claim and attach the price paid for knowing it.

- "This cost me $3,000 to learn."
- "I wasted eight months on the wrong offer."
- "Almost lost the account over that email."
- "We shipped it, then rolled it back in four days."
- "I've made this mistake twice, which is once more than I'd like to admit."

Stakes cannot be faked without lying, and readers know it. That is exactly why
they work.

---

## 3. Sensory replacement

Named emotions are the weakest form of emotional writing. Replace the label with
the physical fact.

| Label | Physical |
|---|---|
| I was stressed | my jaw was tight enough that my teeth hurt |
| it was exciting | I couldn't sit down, kept walking to the kitchen and back |
| I was nervous | I read the message four times before sending it |
| it was awkward | nobody said anything for about six seconds |
| I was exhausted | I fell asleep with the laptop open |

Use sparingly. Two or three per piece. A sensory detail on every paragraph
becomes its own pattern, and an over-described one reads as fiction.

## 4. Process over polish

Machines present conclusions. People show the route.

- The false start, and why it failed
- The version you deleted
- The thing that worked for the wrong reason
- The order you actually did it in, including the backwards part
- The ugly draft
- What you still have not solved

A piece that shows only the finished thinking is indistinguishable from every
other piece that shows only the finished thinking. The mess is the moat.

## 5. Earned uncertainty

There are two kinds of hedging. One is slop, one is credibility.

**Slop hedging** is structural and content-free: "it depends on various
factors," "results may vary," "there are pros and cons to consider," "your
mileage may vary."

**Earned uncertainty** is specific about the limits of what you know:
- "I've only tried this with service businesses. No idea if it holds for
  products."
- "Worked twice. Failed once, with a client who had no list."
- "I think this is why it worked, but I changed two things at once, so I can't
  actually separate them."
- "Still figuring this part out."

Earned uncertainty makes the confident parts more believable, because it proves
you are tracking the difference.

## 6. Rhythm surgery

Mechanical, and it works. Three passes over the draft:

**Pass 1 — break the longest.** Find the three longest consecutive sentences.
Break one into two. Find a place where three short sentences run together and
fuse two with a comma.

**Pass 2 — plant a short one.** Put a sentence of three to five words somewhere
it will land. Once. Not once per paragraph.

**Pass 3 — read aloud.** Where your voice flattens, cut. Where you run out of
breath, break. Where you stumble, the syntax is wrong.

**Targets:** sentence-length standard deviation above 8. Burstiness above 0.55.
At least one sentence under five words and one over twenty-five in anything
longer than 200 words.

**Do not build a new metronome.** If the rhythm becomes long-long-short,
long-long-short, you have replaced one pattern with another.

## 7. Voice transplant

If a Voice DNA profile exists for the operator or client, it outranks the
lexical blacklist. Apply in this order:

1. **Their sentence length distribution.** Some people write in 40-word
   sentences. Do not shorten them into somebody else's rhythm.
2. **Their vocabulary**, including the words they use slightly wrong and the
   ones they overuse. Those are fingerprints.
3. **Their contractions.** Some people never contract. Match them.
4. **Their conviction level.** Calm and measured is a voice. So is loud. Do not
   default to punchy.
5. **Their idiom and references.** The industries they name, the tools they
   mention, the way they swear or do not.
6. **Their structural habits.** Where they put the point. Some bury it. Some
   lead with it.

**The override rule:** if the operator genuinely says "at the end of the day"
in unscripted voice notes, it is their voice, not slop. The lexical gate bends.
The specificity, evidence, structure, and purpose gates do not.

**Without a Voice DNA profile:** write as one specific person talking to one
specific person. Anonymous competence is the default register, and the default
register is the problem.

## 8. The position test

The single fastest way to make a piece not-slop: **say something someone
competent could disagree with.**

Scan every sentence and ask: would anyone in this field push back on this? If
nothing in the piece is arguable, you have written a description, not an
argument, and descriptions of things everyone already accepts are what slop is
made of.

Ways to get a position onto the page:
- Name the thing you think is overrated in your own field
- State the trade-off you accept that others do not
- Say what you stopped doing and why
- Name who your advice is *wrong* for
- Disagree with a specific piece of common advice, on the record

**A note on manufactured contrarianism:** "everything you know about X is wrong"
is not a position, it is a costume. A real position has a cost — it will annoy
someone you would rather not annoy. If your contrarian take is safe, it is not
a take.

## 9. Moves that backfire

**Deliberate typos.** The one humanising tactic readers experience as
sloppiness rather than personality. It costs credibility and buys nothing.

**Over-casualness.** Forcing "lol," "ngl," "tbh," or lowercase-everything onto a
voice that does not have it reads as costume, not character.

**Excessive fragments.** One-word sentences. Everywhere. Like this. Becomes its
own metronome, and on LinkedIn specifically it is now a recognised AI-adjacent
tic.

**Sensory overload.** A physical detail in every paragraph reads as creative
writing exercise.

**Manufactured vulnerability.** "I'll be honest with you" followed by nothing
honest. Vulnerability without a cost is a performance.

**Anti-pattern chasing.** Restructuring a good sentence to dodge an em dash, or
avoiding the word "crucial" so hard that the sentence loses its meaning. Letting
the detector set your style is the same surrender as letting the model write it.

## 10. A full worked rewrite

**Before — passes spellcheck, fails everything else**

> In today's rapidly evolving digital landscape, building a personal brand on
> LinkedIn has become crucial for founders looking to scale their business.
>
> Most people think posting consistently is enough. But the truth is,
> consistency without strategy is just noise.
>
> Here are 3 pillars that will transform your LinkedIn presence:
>
> - Clarify your positioning to attract your ideal clients
> - Create content that resonates with your target audience
> - Convert engagement into meaningful business conversations
>
> Studies show that founders who follow this framework see significantly better
> results. The choice is yours — start implementing these strategies today.
>
> What's holding you back? 🚀

**The diagnosis:** banned opener, four focal words (evolving, landscape,
crucial, resonates), a negation pivot, a forced three-pillar framework with
symmetric bullets all starting with a verb, unsourced "studies show," vague
quantifier, banned closer, banned rhetorical question, decorative emoji. Zero
concretes. Nothing arguable. Substitute any company and it survives intact.

**After**

> A founder I worked with posted every weekday for seven months. Four hundred
> and forty-one followers at the end of it. Two DMs, both recruiters.
>
> She was doing the thing everyone tells you to do.
>
> The problem was her headline. It said "Helping businesses grow through
> innovative solutions." Nobody searches that. Nobody reads it and thinks
> "that's my problem." So the posts went out into a feed of people who had no
> reason to click through, and the ones who did clicked away again.
>
> We changed it to name the actual job: reconciling Xero accounts for agencies
> running 20 to 80 clients. Same posting schedule. Same person writing them.
>
> Eleven inbound conversations in the next six weeks. Three became calls.
>
> I don't think consistency is the problem for most people. I think it's that
> they're consistent about something nobody was looking for. Which is worse,
> because it takes seven months to find out.
>
> If your headline doesn't name a job someone would type into a search bar,
> that's the thing to fix before you write another post.

**What changed:** concretes throughout — seven months, 441 followers, two DMs,
20–80 clients, eleven conversations, three calls. The claim is arguable
(consistency is not the problem) and stated as opinion rather than fact. The
number is unflattering, which is why it reads as real. Sentence lengths swing
from four words to thirty-two. The bullets are gone because there were never
three things — there was one. The CTA is a specific diagnostic action, not a
feeling. Swap the company name and it breaks, because it is about one person on
one Tuesday.
